    1. 01 2010–2011 · Northcentral Technical College

      Tech school, and a T-Rex

      Northcentral Technical College, Network Specialist. My first program ever was an Alice animation called T-Rex vs. Bunny (Fall 2010; the file is still on my NAS). Then Packet Tracer labs, Active Directory, InfoSec, project management, and a lot of cable.

    2. 02 2013 · Charter Communications, Madison

      Turning up fiber by the thousand

      Hired straight out of school onto the fiber enterprise team. Point-to-point circuits, cell backhaul out to carrier MTSOs, off-net Type 2s, and big multipoint E-LANs for customers like a Fortune 500 insurer and a regional grocery chain. Every turn-up tested with EXFO (RFC 2544, Y.1731).

      1,000–2,000 circuits in a good year. My share, not the team's.

    3. 03 ~2016 · The first automation

      5,000 lines of Visual Basic

      Before I knew how to code properly, I built a spreadsheet that drove our CRM through the browser: circuit IDs, IP and VLAN assignment, device allocation, even the Visio diagram. An order that took over an hour took about five minutes. A thirty-person team ended up using it, and management mostly wanted to know why I was suddenly so fast.

    4. 04 2016–2018 · Time Warner merger

      No standards group, so I wrote the standards

      Charter bought Time Warner Cable and our all-Cisco region went multi-vendor overnight. I led the move to Juniper, wrote the internet, voice and WAN standards in Confluence, and built a JavaScript config generator for MX960 and MX480 routers. We were one of two teams, out of about seven, that got off stale spreadsheets. Passed CCNP SWITCH along the way (835, needed 790).

    5. 05 2020 · Python

      Do it by hand until it hurts. Then script it.

      New router standards were landing a few times a week. I did them by hand until I couldn't stand it, then taught myself Python: weekly audits and fixes across the region's Cisco ASR 9000s, Juniper MXs and Nokia 7750s. When the regional QoS policy had to be rewritten on every PE router, I automated it solo. Other regions put five or six engineers on the same job.

    6. 06 2024–2026 · DevOps team

      Tools for engineers, shipped through CI/CD

      I got pulled onto the DevOps team building apps for the network engineering org. On our password-rotation platform (thousands of core routers, hundreds of thousands of customer devices) I wrote the multi-vendor modules: the home-grown Paramiko code that knows how to drive each vendor's CLI. Secrets lived in HashiCorp Vault; GitLab CI/CD promoted releases from candidate to live across three regional servers.

      When I moved over, my old regional work was split across six engineers: three seniors, two NE4s and a principal.

    7. 07 2025 → now · The lab

      Then AI showed up

      In January 2025 I started vibe-coding a garden planner just to learn the AI tools. By spring 2026 I'd bought a Mac Studio to run models locally and was spending half my day in Claude Code. Charter closed my office in June 2026, and I've been building ever since. Everything below this line came out of that.

    A · The Hub

    Mission control for AI agents

    Problem
    A dozen AI agents and Claude Code sessions means a dozen terminals, and no way to approve what an agent wants to do before it does it.
    Built
    One web app on the Mac Studio. Every Claude Code session sits in one workspace, and local models run through Ollama. There are studios for image, video, music and voice, plus an approval queue where agents park prompts until I OK them.
    The AI
    A voice agent drafts the work, the Hub holds it, I approve, and Claude Code does it. Local 122B-parameter models handle anything private or offline.
    Result
    I spend about half my day in it. Every image, song and voice on this page came out of it.

    Flaskgevent + WebSocketsOllamaComfyUIMLXClaude Code

    B · The house platform

    Software for the people I live with

    Problem
    A tornado warning at 3 a.m. can't depend on somebody checking their phone, and the house was a pile of apps that didn't talk to each other.
    Built
    A Flask platform on a Raspberry Pi with radar and severe-weather alerts, Sonos control, smart plugs, AI radio DJs, tasks, meal planning and a recipe book you can dictate to while you cook. There's also an agent API, so AI assistants can use all of it by name.
    The AI
    A local LLM summarizes National Weather Service alerts. A shared voice service called Thaddeus speaks them on every speaker, raises the volume for tornadoes, then puts each room back the way it was. Agents can write recipes freely, but every change gets flagged for a human to review.
    Result
    There are no quiet hours, on purpose: warnings wake the house. Advisories stay silent so nobody learns to tune it out.

    PythonSQLitenginxSonos / UPnPESPHomePiper TTSOpenAPI

    C · Fleet ops

    SolarWinds-class visibility, without SolarWinds

    Problem
    Five machines, two Pis acting as a high-availability Tailscale router pair, a dozen services and their certificates. Failures were silent.
    Built
    An agentless monitor using SNMP and SSH, a maintenance page for patching and reboots, and certificate-expiry alerts. A Repos page checks that the Studio, GitHub, a local Forgejo mirror and production all agree. Deploys are GitOps: push, and every Pi pulls within two minutes.
    The AI
    Claude Code is my pair programmer. It builds and verifies, I set direction and review, and every lesson goes into written notes so the next session starts smarter.
    Result
    Failures are loud now. Default-drop firewalls, keys-only SSH, staggered unattended upgrades and DNS-01 certificates.

    D · Dairy Audit Assistant

    An AI that has to be right, not just plausible

    Problem
    Grade “A” dairy rules span the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance, federal code, Wisconsin's ATCP 65 and years of FDA memos. Here, a wrong citation is worse than no answer.
    Built
    A citation-grounded RAG assistant over current editions only, with a definitions-first source hierarchy and a map of the 247 IMS-listed dairy operations in Wisconsin.
    The AI
    It runs on local models, with a separate decomposition step for scenario questions. The eval suite has both lookups and real scenarios, because a lookup-only eval scored 19/19 while scenarios were badly broken.
    Result
    Recall 17/17, lookups 19/19, scenario coverage 17/17. The lessons: fix structure, not prompts; misattribution is the dangerous failure; and a chunk boundary can make a document say the opposite of what it says.

    E · GrowYear

    You get one garden per year

    Planning a garden means spreadsheets, sketches and five seed catalogs. I'm not that organized, so I needed entry and planning to be dead simple.

    07 · How I work

    Seven habits, in my own words.

    01

    Do it by hand until it hurts. Then script it.

    I'm detail-oriented, but I can't do the same task every day. So I don't. I learn it by hand, then I automate it. I did that for three years before I knew how to code, and it's been all systems go since.

    02

    First one in.

    A new device, a new process, the thing everybody's scared to touch: that's where I want to be. If you need a trailblazer, I'm your guy.

    03

    Hammer and duct tape, then the real fix.

    When it's on fire, I fix it now. Then I come back and fix it right, and write down what happened.

    04

    Make failures loud.

    Silent failures are the worst kind. Everything I run has a health check and an alarm, and a warning beats a quiet fallback every time.

    05

    Agents flag. Humans approve.

    Let AI write freely where it's reversible, and make it ask before it acts. That gets you speed where it's safe and a gate where it isn't.

    06

    Write it down for somebody else.

    I document because I forget things, and writing for an audience forces me to do it well. At Charter I was the team's Confluence guy.

    07

    Small teams, face to face.

    I do my best work on a small team, with one-on-ones over group calls and a hands-off manager who notices good work. A “nice job” goes a long way with me.
