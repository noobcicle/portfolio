      1. 01 2010–2012 · Northcentral Technical College

        Tech school

        An AAS in IT Network Specialist (Unified Communications), plus a technical certificate in website hardware and software administration. Graduated in December 2012.

      2. 02 2013 · Charter Communications, Madison

        Turning up thousands of circuits

        My first real job in tech: Charter's enterprise fiber team. Point-to-point circuits, cell backhaul out to carrier switching offices, off-net circuits through other carriers, and sprawling multipoint networks for customers like a Fortune 500 insurer and a regional grocery chain. Every turn-up got tested before I'd call it done.

        At the start, every one was handcrafted: each config built by hand in a Notepad file. Copy, paste, edit, repeat. No automation at all. That's the before.

      3. 03 ~2016 · The first automation

        So I resorted to Visual Basic

        Copy and paste only scales so far. My first real program was a spreadsheet that drove our order system through a web browser and filled in circuit IDs, IPs, VLANs, device assignments, even the Visio diagram. I wrote it like a first-timer: no proper functions, no methods, mostly if statements, all in one giant, bloated script. It worked anyway. Before long the whole team was using it, and management mostly wanted to know why I was suddenly so fast.

      4. 04 2016–2018 · The Time Warner merger

        Nobody owned the standards, so I wrote them

        Charter bought Time Warner Cable and our all-Cisco region woke up multi-vendor. The standards were a mess: everybody kept their own Excel sheet, and no two matched. Somebody had to write it all down where the whole team could share it, so I did. I picked up JavaScript and built a tool that spit out Juniper MX960 and MX480 configs on demand, became our Confluence admin, and wrote the customer circuit standards for internet, voice and WAN.

        That standards work turned into a team-lead role: documentation, and making sure everybody had the information they needed. Somewhere in there I also earned my CCNP.

      5. 05 2020 · Python

        Then I taught myself Python

        New router standards were landing a few times a week. I did them by hand until I couldn't stand it, then taught myself Python. My scripts audited and fixed the region's Cisco ASR 9000s, Juniper MXs and Nokia 7750s every week, and when the regional QoS policy needed rewriting on every PE router, I rewrote it and automated the rollout.

      6. 06 2020 on · Migrations

        Send me the cut sheet. Press play.

        Migrations were where the hours went: hundreds of sites, and tasks that took an engineer hours by hand. So they'd send me the cut sheet as an Excel file, and my script built the whole method of procedure: SSH into the device, pull the config, analyze it, pull out every variable that mattered, and build the config for the destination device. Basically, press play.

        Each migration saved hundreds of hours. Over thirteen years, thousands at least, because for most of a decade, making everything faster was basically the whole job.

      7. 07 2023–2026 · DevOps team

        Tools for the whole engineering org

        Then I got pulled onto the DevOps team, building apps for the network engineering org. I was a lead developer on our network inventory and security platform: one product in two parts. Discovery crawled the network live over SSH, through PE routers, management VRFs and gateway interfaces, found each device, profiled its vendor, model and code, and stored it in a SQL inventory (MariaDB, later PostgreSQL). Security modules then ran against everything discovery found: password rotation and auditing, across hundreds of thousands of devices.

        I built the inventory database and wrote the multi-vendor modules: the home-grown Paramiko code that knows how to talk to each vendor's CLI, quirks and all. Secrets lived in HashiCorp Vault, and the code shipped through GitLab.

      8. 08 2024–2026 · AI at work

        AI showed up at work first

        Before AI took over my basement, it showed up on the job. Our team paired GitLab with Amazon Q in the IDE, and I was the one who hooked the two together. I also worked in Kiro, Amazon's AI for the terminal. That was enterprise AI: real code, and a real network on the other end. Everything I build at home builds on it.

      9. 09 2026 → now · The basement

        Then I brought it home

        In early 2026 I started vibe-coding a garden planner, mostly to find out what the hype was about. By spring there was a Mac Studio in my basement running AI models around the clock, and I was spending half my day in Claude Code. The garden got a planner, and the planner is now live on the internet. The house got a voice. It escalated.

      10. 10 June 2026 · The plot twist

        Busy week

        Tuesday, my kid was born. Friday, Charter closed my office. I took some time to be a dad, then went back down to the basement and built most of what you're about to scroll through.

      The app
      React 18 and TypeScript, built with Vite: Tailwind, Radix UI (shadcn/ui), TanStack Query and Zustand. It's a static bundle that nginx serves.
      The back end
      Supabase, trimmed to the three parts it needs, in Docker: Postgres, GoTrue for sign-in and PostgREST for the API. About half a gigabyte of a 2 GB Pi.
      Sign-in and data
      Google sign-in only. Row-level security in Postgres decides every read and write: your garden is yours, and the seed catalog is read-only to everyone else.
      Where it runs
      My second Raspberry Pi, in the basement, at mygrowyear.com. A Cloudflare Tunnel carries the traffic, so nothing at home is open to the internet.
      Shipping it
      One command from the Mac Studio: typecheck, tests, build, database migrations, an atomic switch to the new release, then a health check that puts the old one back if anything fails.
      Keeping it
      Nightly database backups, copied to another machine in the house, and a monitor that checks its public address every two minutes.

      08 · Why I build

      About sixty gardens.

      Plants want to succeed. Leave a router alone long enough and one day it just stops working. Give a pepper plant sun, water and a little tending, and it keeps on growing.

      The garden is where I go to get out of my tech. Every January I plan the next one like it's the first, and by August it's a jungle.

      None of it is meant to last. Each garden is here for a season, and that's the point. A gardener who starts young gets maybe sixty gardens in a lifetime. Not even a hundred.

      That's why I build tools: so the computers get more of the chores, and my family gets more of me.

      09 · How I work

      The 7 habits of a mildly effective engineer.

      01

      Twice by hand. The third time, a script.

      I'm detail-oriented, but give me the same task every day and I'll automate it out of spite. I did things the hard way for about three years before I learned to code. It's been all systems go since.

      02

      First one in.

      A new device, a new process, the thing everybody's scared to touch: point me at it. I'd rather blaze the trail than babysit it.

      03

      Hammer and duct tape, then the real fix.

      When it's on fire, I fix it now. Then I come back with a plan, fix it right, and write down what happened so it doesn't happen again.

      04

      Make failures loud.

      Silent failures are the worst kind. Everything I run has a health check and an alarm, and a loud warning beats a quiet workaround every time.

      05

      The AI asks first.

      AI can write all day where mistakes are easy to undo. Anything that actually does something waits for a human to say yes. Fast where it's safe, careful where it counts.

      06

      Write it down for somebody else.

      I document everything because I forget everything. Writing for an audience makes it good. At Charter that turned into being the team's Confluence admin.

      07

      Small team, real conversations.

      I do my best work on a small team, with one-on-ones instead of forty-person calls and a manager who lets me run but notices good work. A “nice job” buys a lot of mileage with me.

      The server, right now

      A Raspberry Pi 4 with 2 GB of RAM, in my basement.

      And the sidebar? It's a traceroute of your own request, told one hop per section. Your request really did reach Cloudflare (the data center is the one that answered you), then came down a tunnel over Starlink to the Pi. My house has no public IPv4 address: Starlink puts it behind carrier-grade NAT, so nothing on the internet can dial in. Instead, cloudflared on the Pi dials out to Cloudflare over IPv6 and holds four encrypted connections open, and your request rides one of them home. The first half of the trip is simulated: the ISP, the transit networks and every metric are made up, but IS-IS really runs Dijkstra and BGP really walks its best-path rules, right in your browser.
